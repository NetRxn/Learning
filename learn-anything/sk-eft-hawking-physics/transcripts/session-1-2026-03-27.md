## Act 1: Acoustic Metric and Horizons

**Instructor:** Imagine you're a sound wave — a little density ripple — living inside a flowing fluid. When the flow speed v < c_s, you can propagate freely. But at the sonic point where v = c_s, you're swimming as fast as you can against a current moving just as fast. You're stuck. That sonic horizon is, for all practical purposes, a black hole horizon — information downstream can never reach upstream.

The mathematical fact: if you write down the wave equation for small perturbations in a moving fluid — linearize Euler around a background flow — you get exactly the wave equation for a massless scalar field on curved spacetime. Mathematically identical. The acoustic metric has the fluid velocity playing the role of gravitational potential and the speed of sound as the local light speed.

## Act 2: Hawking Radiation from Horizons

**Instructor:** Hawking's insight, transplanted into our fluid: a horizon creates particles. Quantum vacuum fluctuations near the horizon produce virtual pairs. One partner falls through the horizon; the other escapes. They can't recombine. The survivor becomes a real phonon with thermal spectrum:

T_H = ℏκ / (2π k_B)

where κ is the surface gravity — how fast the flow speed changes at the horizon. But here's what should bother you: this derivation assumes the low-energy theory holds all the way down to arbitrarily short distances. The escaping phonon, traced backward, was once exponentially blue-shifted — pushed to frequencies far above what a fluid can support. This is the trans-Planckian problem. The miracle is that the thermal spectrum appears robust. The UV physics washes out. But does it wash out *perfectly*, or does it leave fingerprints?

**Assessment:** The learner now understands the trans-Planckian problem not as an abstract formal issue, but as a concrete physical challenge: information about short-distance physics is encoded in which virtual pairs escape. This motivates the entire dissipative correction program.

## Act 3: Viscosity and Dissipation

**Instructor:** A real fluid has viscosity. Molecules collide, momentum diffuses, energy dissipates. Viscosity breaks time-reversal symmetry — Euler equations are reversible; Navier-Stokes are not. Dissipation picks an arrow of time. This means the Hawking calculation — which relies on time-reversal properties — must get corrected. Viscosity should modify Hawking radiation. But how?

You can't just "add viscosity" to the Hawking derivation. The standard argument uses the S-matrix — infinite past to infinite future. But dissipation means the system is open. Energy leaks into thermal fluctuations. There's no clean S-matrix anymore.

## Act 4: The Schwinger-Keldysh Formalism

**Instructor:** In ordinary quantum mechanics, to compute a probability you use one copy of time evolution: ⟨B|A⟩. But to compute an expectation value in a thermal system — "what's the average number of phonons emitted right now?" — you need two copies: one forward, one backward. That's the forward and backward branches of the SK contour.

In a dissipative system, the forward and backward evolutions aren't identical. Dissipation treats them differently. This difference generates the noise terms in the effective action. Dissipation and noise are the two faces of the same coin.

The fields decompose into:
- r-fields (average of forward and backward): the physical, classical-like degrees of freedom
- a-fields (difference): the noise/quantum fluctuation degrees of freedom

The KMS condition — the statement that the system is in thermal equilibrium — relates dissipation and noise by a factor e^{βω}. This is the fluctuation-dissipation theorem elevated to a symmetry principle.

**Assessment:** The instructor conveys the essential structure of SK formalism: two-branch accounting, noise-dissipation duality, and KMS as a symmetry constraint. The physical motivation for each ingredient is clear.

## Act 5: The Three Axioms and the 9→2 Reduction

**Instructor:** You want the most general SK effective action for a dissipative fluid near a sonic horizon. You write every term allowed by symmetry, organized by derivative count (derivative expansion). But SK comes with three axioms:

1. Normalization: path integral equals 1 when sources are zero (probability is conserved)
2. Positivity: imaginary part of action ≥ 0 (entropy never decreases)
3. KMS condition: action respects thermal equilibrium relation between noise and dissipation

For first-order gradient terms (one derivative), there are naively 9 candidate terms. Impose the three axioms:

- Normalization kills terms purely in the r-sector
- KMS relates dissipation and noise coefficients, eliminating free parameters
- Positivity constrains the signs

Result: 9 terms → 2 independent terms. Both proportional to shear viscosity.

The final correction to Hawking flux:

δ_diss = Γ_H / κ

where Γ_H is the dissipation rate at the horizon and κ is the surface gravity.

**Assessment:** This is the punchline. The instructor has shown that symmetries force a specific form — it's not numerology but necessity. Dimensional analysis plus SK axioms completely constrain the answer.

## Topic: KMS Condition and Fluctuation-Dissipation

**Instructor:** Here's the physical setup. You're in a warm room at temperature T. Two experiments:

Experiment 1 (Response): Flick the air — tiny disturbance. Measure how it dissipates. Characterize by retarded Green's function G_R(ω).

The remarkable fact: in thermal equilibrium, these are locked together:

G_noise(ω) = coth(ω/2T) × Im[G_R(ω)]

This is the fluctuation-dissipation relation. Spontaneous noise and dissipative response are the same physics viewed from different angles. Why? What forces this?

The KMS condition. In thermal equilibrium, there's a specific relationship between correlation functions: ⟨A(t) B(0)⟩ = ⟨B(0) A(t+iβ)⟩, where the imaginary time shift is iβ = i/T. This imaginary time periodicity is the entire content of KMS.

**Instructor:** Great — exactly the right thing to be confused about. Your instinct to want a picture is spot on. Let me build one.

Your first guess — repeated vibration of atoms — is actually closer than you think, but the periodicity isn't in real time. It's in a direction perpendicular to real time.

In the complex time plane, there are two directions you can move:

**Horizontal (real time):** This is normal physics. The system evolves via e^{-iHt}. The i in the exponent means everything oscillates. Phases spin around. Energy states don't get preferred — there's no decay. This is just the universe running forward.

**Vertical (imaginary time, τ):** Set t = -iτ, where τ is real. The evolution operator becomes e^{-Hτ}. The i is gone. Pure exponential decay. A high-energy state gets suppressed like e^{-E·τ}. Low-energy states survive. High-energy states get exponentially crushed.

So moving in the imaginary-time direction is like applying a filter that preferentially suppresses high-energy states.

Now the punchline: make it periodic. Move in the imaginary-time direction by exactly τ = β = 1/T. The evolution operator is e^{-βH}. That's — divide by normalization — the thermal density matrix. The Boltzmann distribution. Thermal equilibrium at temperature T.

Now glue the endpoints together. Declare that τ = 0 and τ = β are the same point. Imaginary time becomes a circle of circumference β = 1/T. Real time runs along the axis; imaginary time wraps around — a cylinder.

Going once around this circle computes Tr[e^{-βH} ...] — the thermal trace. The periodicity in imaginary time is the mathematical definition of a thermal state. Every thermal correlation function must be periodic in this imaginary-time direction with period β. That periodicity *is* the KMS condition.

Why does this constrain everything? Any correlation function on the cylinder can be decomposed into Fourier modes around the circle. The periodicity forces a specific relationship between the Fourier coefficients. In frequency space, that relationship is exactly the fluctuation-dissipation relation: noise at frequency ω is locked to dissipation at frequency ω by a factor involving e^{βω}. The factor e^{βω} comes directly from going once around the imaginary-time circle — it's the Boltzmann weight for that frequency.

Hot system (large T, small β): the circle is tiny. The constraint is very tight — almost no room between noise and dissipation. Practically equal. That's why hot systems have the simple relation: noise ∝ dissipation.

Cold system (small T, large β): the circle is huge. More room for quantum effects to create asymmetry.

Back to your intuition: your first instinct — "repeated vibration of atoms" — was touching something real. The atoms are fluctuating thermally, and KMS says those thermal fluctuations have a very specific structure. But the periodicity isn't in real time. It's in this imaginary-time direction — a mathematical direction that encodes the statistical properties of the thermal state.

Think of it this way: **temperature wraps time into a circle, and the circumference of that circle is 1/T.**
